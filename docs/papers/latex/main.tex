\begin{abstract}
The Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) controller shipped with ROS~2
Nav2 is a widely used deployable local controller for mobile robots, yet
in narrow, crowded, dynamic spaces it can stall in local minima, and its
critics avoid collisions only through soft costs, with no formal safety
guarantee. Methods exist for each weakness, and several works outside
Nav2 address both together, but we know of no public Nav2 controller
plugin that combines local-minima escape with a barrier-based safety
filter. This system paper presents Safe-Escape MPPI (SE-MPPI), a Nav2
controller plugin that integrates online local-minima detection-and-escape
for MPPI with a control-barrier-function (CBF) safety filter for tracked
dynamic obstacles. A first version of the plugin reached the goal in none
of its 44 valid live trials on a correct build; we report the four
defects that we identify as the cause (one inferred, not logged) and the
repaired controller. Its escape carries four additions to the advised
design: two of them, avoid lists and a movement watchdog, were needed for
the development sweeps of a family of pockets to pass (ablated jointly; the
ablation is confounded with position in the sweep), and two are safeguards
for other configurations. We disclose all four, with a joint ablation of the
first two. In a pre-registered live campaign (one post hoc amendment to its
crash-stop rule, after which all s4 SE-MPPI trials ran), SE-MPPI without
recoveries reached the goal
in 5 of 6 on s1 (Wilson 95\% interval $[0.44, 0.97]$) and 5 of 6 on the
held-out s4, which lies outside the development family; stock without
recoveries in 0 of 6 on each; with Nav2's default recovery tree, on s1,
stock 0 of 6 and SE-MPPI 3 of 6, on one simulated host
(Fisher exact $p=0.0152$ for each of the first two contrasts and $p=0.1818$
for the third, which is descriptive). s1 is the development scenario; s4
carries the claim. A third pocket, s3, that has a near mirror twin in the
development family gave 4 of 6 against 0 of 6 ($p=0.0606$, not
significant). We make no claim beyond one simulated host, six
repetitions per cell, and no real robot. In a randomized, paired
$1{,}200$-trial benchmark on a Python 2D mirror that transcribes the
shipped algorithm, not on the Nav2 controller itself, escape raises
success on the static U-trap family from $0\%$ to $80\%$ (Wilson 95\%
interval $[67,89]$); without the four additions the same benchmark gives
$58\%$. The escape--safety coordination matches an independent escape+CBF
stack there, a null result, so we claim it only as a mechanism. By a
standard argument for time-varying CBF gains, the gain schedule
preserves forward invariance of the barrier's safe set, a conditional
guarantee that covers only a look-ahead point and the tracked dynamic
obstacles, assumes that the barrier inequality holds between control
updates, and requires a slack-free quadratic program.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Autonomous mobile robots increasingly operate in cluttered, dynamic indoor
environments -- warehouses, hospitals, service floors -- where the local
controller must be simultaneously \emph{fast}, \emph{non-conservative}, and
\emph{safe}. Within the ROS~2 Nav2 ecosystem, the MPPI controller
(\texttt{nav2\_mppi\_controller}) \cite{nav2} is a widely used
high-performance option: it supports differential, omnidirectional, and
Ackermann robots and works by sampling \texttt{batch\_size}
rollouts, scoring them with a bank of critics, and combining them with an
information-theoretic softmax \cite{mppi_orig}.

Two weaknesses, however, recur exactly in the environments that matter
most:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Local-minima entrapment.} A finite prediction horizon (by
  default $56 \times 0.05\,\mathrm{s} \approx 2.8\,\mathrm{s}$) and
  zero-mean Gaussian sampling cause the robot to stall or oscillate in
  non-convex traps. Within the MPPI critic set, Nav2's mitigations
  (\texttt{PreferForwardCritic}, \texttt{TwirlingCritic}) are
  \emph{always-on} cost shaping that neither \emph{detects} entrapment nor
  steers an escape. At the stack level, Nav2 does detect a stall: the
  controller server's progress checker reports a failure when the robot
  has not moved a set distance within a set time, and the default behavior
  tree then clears the costmaps and runs generic recoveries such as
  spinning, waiting, and backing up \cite{nav2,nav2_progress,nav2_bt}.
  These recoveries are not directed toward an opening. In the MPPI
  literature, one line of work reshapes the sampling distribution all the
  time, in part to reduce entrapment \cite{log_mppi,biased_mppi,flow_mppi}
  and in part for other aims \cite{tsallis_mppi,svg_mppi}; another adds
  guidance: GP-MPPI recommends a free-space subgoal from a learned
  navigability surface \cite{gp_mppi}, and repulsive-potential
  augmentation adds an artificial potential field to the MPPI cost
  \cite{drpa_sii}, which DRPA-MPPI switches on only when it detects
  entrapment \cite{drpa_mppi}. None of these carries a safety certificate.
\item \textbf{No formal safety.} In Nav2 MPPI, avoidance is encoded as
  soft costs (\texttt{ObstaclesCritic}, \texttt{CostCritic}); there is no
  forward-invariance guarantee, and crucially \textbf{no prediction of
  dynamic-obstacle motion} -- the costmap is a present-time snapshot and
  \texttt{collision\_monitor} is purely reactive. CBF--MPPI fusions supply
  safety \cite{shield_mppi,br_mppi,scbf_mppi,dualguard,gs_mppi}, but none
  of them detects entrapment or adds an escape mechanism (GS-MPPI counters
  the myopic behavior often associated with CBFs through long-term
  performance terms), and none ships as a Nav2 controller. Among CBF
  integrations with Nav2 that we could verify, the Nav2 documentation
  describes inserting a third-party product, the 3Laws Supervisor, which
  its vendor describes as based on CBFs, into the command chain after the
  controller \cite{nav2_3laws,threelaws_pr}; it is a separate node rather
  than a controller plugin, and neither source describes a mechanism for
  escaping local minima.
\end{enumerate}

These weaknesses also interact: being conservative for safety invites
entrapment, and escaping aggressively invites collision. Several works
outside Nav2 and MPPI address both at once, among them model predictive
control that escapes local minima while keeping a safe backup trajectory
\cite{soloperto}, gap-based local planners with provable collision-free
properties \cite{potential_gap,safer_gap,dynamic_gap}, and modulated CBF
filters \cite{mcbf_qp} that reduce or eliminate, among moving obstacles,
the spurious equilibria that CBF quadratic programs (QPs) can introduce
\cite{reis_cbf_equilibria}, with concurrent extensions
\cite{xue_proactive,chen_milp_cbf}. In SE-MPPI, the entrapment detector is driven by progress along a
per-task anchor path and runs inside the controller rather than at the stack
level; Appendix~\ref{app:v1} keeps a standalone 2D illustration of the design
stage. This is a system contribution, and we state it as
such.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 (system).} A public \textbf{Nav2 local-controller
  plugin} that combines online local-minima detection-and-escape with a
  CBF safety filter for tracked dynamic obstacles, packaged as a
  \texttt{nav2\_core::Controller} plus a pluginlib MPPI critic. Each
  ingredient has precedent: gated repulsion in DRPA-MPPI
  \cite{drpa_mppi}, free-space subgoal and gap guidance in GP-MPPI
  \cite{gp_mppi} and gap-based planners \cite{potential_gap,safer_gap},
  CBF filtering of MPPI commands \cite{scbf_mppi}, and a supervisor for
  Nav2 that its vendor describes as CBF-based
  \cite{nav2_3laws,threelaws_pr}. What is new is their integration inside
  one Nav2 controller; to our knowledge no public controller plugin
  already provides it.
\item \textbf{C2 (repair and live evidence).} The first version of the
  plugin did not reach the goal in a live simulated stack. We name the
  four defects behind that (Sec.~\ref{sec:history}), describe the repaired
  controller that ships (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}), disclose the four
  additions to the advised escape (two needed together for the development
  sweeps to pass, ablated jointly; two safeguards for other configurations), and test
  it in a pre-registered live campaign on one simulated host, with one post hoc
  amendment to its crash-stop rule after which all s4 SE-MPPI trials ran
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:campaign2}). The campaign supports a narrow claim: the
  repaired controller without Nav2 recoveries reached the goal in 5 of 6
  trials on the development pocket and in 5 of 6 on a held-out pocket
  outside the development family, where stock MPPI reached it in none.
\item \textbf{C3 (algorithm).} An \textbf{entrapment-gated gain
  schedule}: a rule that raises the CBF class-$\mathcal{K}$ gain on
  detected entrapment and drops it back on an imminent time-to-collision
  (TTC). Online adaptation of CBF gains is established, including
  selecting class-$\mathcal{K}$ parameters to reduce predicted deadlock
  \cite{kim_adaptive_cbf} and letting MPPI optimize a barrier-rate
  parameter \cite{br_mppi}; our rule is a simpler, rule-based two-level
  instance whose specific elements are its trigger and its TTC override.
  Its invariance property (Sec.~\ref{sec:coordination}) is a known result
  for time-varying class-$\mathcal{K}$ terms \cite{ada_cbf,rt_cbf}, which
  we restate as a conditional lemma: it covers the look-ahead point and
  the tracked dynamic obstacles, assumes the barrier inequality holds
  between samples, and requires a slack-free QP. In our 2D benchmark the
  schedule is empirically indistinguishable from an independent
  escape+CBF stack (Sec.~\ref{sec:evsf}), and the live campaign does not
  test it: its pockets contain no moving body (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2results}
  reports the phantom obstacles and forced stops that occurred
  nevertheless, which were not a designed test of the coordination).
\item \textbf{C4 (dynamic safety).} Integration of a look-ahead-point CBF
  safety filter for a differential-drive robot, fed by a costmap-based
  dynamic-obstacle tracker, as a QP at the controller's output. The live
  campaign ran it with the occupancy-based static/dynamic classification, the
  horizon prediction and the conformal margin at their code defaults (on); the
  2D mirror has none of the three (Sec.~\ref{sec:tracking}).
\item \textbf{C5 (reproducibility).} An open Apache-2.0
  implementation,\footnote{Code, benchmark scripts and the campaign
  pre-registration: \url{https://github.com/kjungmo/se-mppi-nav2}
  (Apache-2.0); the raw trial data of campaign 2 are the archive
  \texttt{experiments/campaign2/raw\_trials.tar.gz}; see the Appendix for
  the commands. At the time of writing, the tag, the commits and the archive
  cited in this paper exist only in the author's local repository. A release
  ref will be published before submission; the commit of the local tag
  \texttt{campaign2-controller} (\texttt{6a50723}) is the provenance.} a Python 2D mirror of the shipped
  algorithm with a randomized $1{,}200$-trial benchmark and paired
  significance tests, a headless closed-loop development test with a
  written family rule, and the frozen, hash-checked pre-registration of
  the live campaign with its amendments.
\end{itemize}

We are deliberately conservative about novelty and about what we claim
empirically. Fusing CBFs with MPPI is \emph{not} new, and neither is gated
escape, online adaptation of the CBF gain, or treating escape and safety
together; the paper is best read as a system report on putting these
pieces into one Nav2 controller, on what went wrong the first time, and on
a small pre-registered live test of the repaired version. The live claim
is limited to what the pre-registered branch wording allows
(Sec.~\ref{sec:campaign2}): counts out of six, Fisher exact $p$ values and
Wilson intervals, on one simulated host. In the 2D mirror of the shipped
algorithm the escape layer is the measured effect on the trap families
(Sec.~\ref{sec:bench2d}). The escape--safety coordination (C3) has the
conditional invariance property of Sec.~\ref{sec:coordination} yet, in the
regime we could test, is empirically indistinguishable from an independent
escape+CBF stack; we report this null openly. We ran no real robot, no
third-party escape baseline, and no full-scale physics benchmark
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{MPPI and local controllers.} Nav2 MPPI \cite{nav2,mppi_orig}, TEB
\cite{teb}, DWB (a Nav2 DWA-family planner) \cite{dwa}, and Regulated Pure
Pursuit \cite{rpp} are the standard deployable local planners. MPPI's
critic-plugin interface (\texttt{mppi::critics::CriticFunction}) exposes
per-rollout trajectories, the reference path, the goal, collision flags,
and the furthest-reached path index -- i.e., the signals needed for
entrapment detection are already available, and a custom critic can be
inserted without forking the optimizer. Around the controller, Nav2
already handles stalls at the stack level: the controller server runs a
progress checker, by default \texttt{SimpleProgressChecker}, which reports
a failure when the robot has not moved a set radius within a set time
allowance, and the default behavior tree answers with costmap clearing,
spinning, waiting, and backing up \cite{nav2_progress,nav2_bt}. SE-MPPI's
detector differs in that it runs inside the controller on progress along
the path and steers the escape instead of triggering generic recoveries;
the two mechanisms can coexist. 
\textbf{Local-minima escape.} Most MPPI variants that bear on local minima
are \emph{always-on} changes of the sampling distribution: log-MPPI
\cite{log_mppi} and Biased-MPPI \cite{biased_mppi} name entrapment as a
motivation and learned sampling distributions \cite{flow_mppi} mention poor
local optima, whereas Tsallis-divergence MPC \cite{tsallis_mppi} and
SVG-MPPI \cite{svg_mppi} change the distribution for other reasons. Others
add guidance. GP-MPPI learns a sparse-Gaussian-process model of the
navigable space around the robot and recommends a free-space subgoal to
the local MPPI planner, which lets the robot escape local minima without a
global map \cite{gp_mppi}. Fuke et~al.\ add a repulsive artificial
potential to MPPI to avoid local minima without global path guidance
\cite{drpa_sii}, and their DRPA-MPPI detects potential entrapment on the
predicted trajectories and only then switches to a cost with repulsion
away from the local minimum \cite{drpa_mppi}. DRPA-MPPI is the closest
\emph{detect-and-switch} method and GP-MPPI the closest subgoal method;
neither has a CBF or other formal safety layer, and neither is
Nav2-integrated. SE-MPPI combines both ideas in a simpler form, a
path-progress stall detector gating a classical repulsive potential
\cite{khatib_apf} and a ray-cast gap bearing, and its safety filter runs
alongside the escape rather than gating it. Gap-based local planners are
closer still to the overall architecture. Potential Gap combines
gap-based local navigation with artificial potential fields and restores
provable collision-free properties for realistic robot models
\cite{potential_gap}; Safer Gap adds nonlinear model predictive control
with keyhole zeroing-barrier-function constraints and a point-wise
optimization-based safety filter as the final layer \cite{safer_gap}; and
Dynamic Gap extends provably collision-free gap-based navigation to
dynamic environments by modeling how gaps evolve over time
\cite{dynamic_gap}. Relative to this line, SE-MPPI uses a gap only as a
gated escape heuristic inside MPPI, and its barrier covers only tracked
dynamic obstacles.

\textbf{Escape and safety together.} Several works treat entrapment and
safety jointly. Soloperto et~al.\ optimize an exploration trajectory
together with a safe backup trajectory in a model predictive controller
and prove convergence, feasibility, and constraint satisfaction under
partially unknown constraints \cite{soloperto}. The modulated CBF-QP of
Xue and Figueroa reduces or eliminates, in dynamic environments, the
spurious equilibria that CBF-QP filters create \cite{reis_cbf_equilibria},
and was tested on Ridgeback and Fetch robots \cite{mcbf_qp}. Concurrent
work adds learned predictive barriers for moving concave obstacles to this
filter \cite{xue_proactive}, and pairs a mixed-integer MPC planner with a
Minkowski-difference CBF filter to mitigate the local-minimum behavior of
reactive CBF navigation in U-shaped and maze-like environments
\cite{chen_milp_cbf}. These methods mitigate or avoid minima inside the
planner or the barrier itself, whereas SE-MPPI leaves the barrier
unchanged and escapes through the MPPI cost. None of them is packaged as
a Nav2 controller.

\textbf{CBF~$\times$~MPPI safety.} Three fusion styles exist as research
code: CBF conditions in the rollout cost or the sampling (Shield-MPPI
\cite{shield_mppi}, which adds a local repair step, and BR-MPPI
\cite{br_mppi}, which imposes the CBF condition as an equality with a
parametric linear class-$\mathcal{K}$ function, augments the state with
its parameter, and lets MPPI optimize the parameter's rate as an extra
input), an output CBF-QP filter on the MPPI command (SCBF-MPPI
\cite{scbf_mppi}, built on the CBFKit toolbox \cite{cbfkit}), and
per-rollout safety filtering that makes every sample provably safe
(GS-MPPI \cite{gs_mppi}, DualGuard \cite{dualguard}, which also filters
the final command). For dynamic obstacles, DPCBF \cite{dpcbf} uses a
parabolic safe set that is less conservative than collision-cone barriers
but is QP-only and not embedded in MPPI. Our filter builds on the
classical CBF-QP \cite{ames_cbf_tac,ames_cbf_ecc}. None of these works
ships as a Nav2 controller (CBFKit connects to ROS through its own wrapper module, not through Nav2's
controller interface), and none
detects entrapment or adds an escape mechanism, although GS-MPPI counters
the myopic behavior often associated with CBFs through long-term
performance terms \cite{gs_mppi}. Among Nav2 integrations of a CBF that
we could verify, the official documentation describes inserting the
third-party 3Laws Supervisor, which its vendor describes as based on
CBFs, between the velocity smoother and the collision monitor
\cite{nav2_3laws,threelaws_pr}; it acts on the controller's output as a
separate node, and neither source describes a local-minimum escape
mechanism. SE-MPPI instead places its filter inside the controller and
couples it to the escape layer through its gain.

\textbf{Online adaptation of the CBF gain.} Time-varying and adapted
class-$\mathcal{K}$ terms are well studied. Adaptive CBFs multiply the
class-$\mathcal{K}$ functions by time-varying penalty functions and prove
forward invariance of the safe set \cite{ada_cbf}; optimal-decay CBF-QPs
optimize the decay rate point-wise in time to keep the QP feasible
\cite{optdecay_cbf}; and rate-tunable CBFs adapt the class-$\mathcal{K}$
functions online and derive conditions under which multiple constraints
can share the control input \cite{rt_cbf}. Closest to our trigger, Kim,
Kee, and Panagou adapt the parameters of input-constrained CBFs online
with a learned ensemble model and select, among the candidates that pass
their uncertainty and risk checks, the class-$\mathcal{K}$ parameters with
the minimum predicted deadlock time \cite{kim_adaptive_cbf}; BR-MPPI lets
MPPI optimize the rate of the class-$\mathcal{K}$ parameter as an extra
control input \cite{br_mppi}.
Concurrent work switches nominal objectives when CBF and control-Lyapunov
constraints conflict, to reduce slowdowns and deadlocks
\cite{walia_conflict}, and schedules the gain of a frontier barrier on an
occupancy grid by how well the map is explored \cite{paudel_dualbarrier}.
SE-MPPI's coordinator is a rule-based, two-level instance of this idea,
triggered by the entrapment flag and overridden by time-to-collision, and
its invariance property (Sec.~\ref{sec:coordination}) follows the same
argument as the relative-degree-one case of the adaptive-CBF result
\cite[Thm.~3]{ada_cbf}, there stated for Lipschitz controllers.

\textbf{Conformal prediction for safe control (background, not claimed).}
The released controller carries an online conformal margin that inflates
the CBF effective radius under prediction uncertainty; this is an
implementation detail deferred to follow-up work
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}, SE-Predict) and is \emph{not} a contribution of
this paper. That margin draws on the established line of
conformal-prediction-for-safe-control work
\cite{acp_yang,conformal_hri,lindemann,dixit,ua_pcbf}; the claims of this
paper (C1--C4) stand without it. The live campaign ran with the margin
enabled (Sec.~\ref{sec:tracking}).

\textbf{Positioning.} Table~\ref{tab:positioning} places the reviewed
systems against four factors: \textbf{(a)}~shipping as a Nav2 controller
plugin, \textbf{(b)}~online local-minimum escape or avoidance,
\textbf{(c)}~an explicit run-time safety constraint, filter or backup, and
\textbf{(d)}~online adaptation of the class-$\mathcal{K}$ gain. No
reviewed system holds all four, but several hold two, fully or in part:
stock Nav2 pairs its controller plugins with stack-level stall recovery,
gap-based planners, backup-trajectory MPC and modulated CBFs combine
local-minimum handling with a safety layer, and adaptive-gain CBFs and
BR-MPPI pair a barrier with gain adaptation, with deadlock-aware
adaptation also holding the second factor in part. The delta of SE-MPPI
is therefore the conjunction inside one Nav2 controller plugin, not any
single element. Its own marks are not all full: its barrier covers only
tracked dynamic obstacles, and its escape is demonstrated live only in
simulation, on one host, with six repetitions per cell, and in the Python 2D mirror. Its gain rule differs from prior gain adaptation mainly
in its trigger, and, as Sec.~\ref{sec:evsf} shows, we could not measure
an outcome benefit from it.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Consider a differential-drive robot with pose $(x,y,\theta)$ and control
$u=(v,\omega)$, tracking a global path $\sigma$ produced by a Nav2 global
planner, amid static structure and a set $\mathcal{O}$ of dynamic
obstacles $o$ with position $p_o$, radius $R_o$, and (estimated) velocity
$v_o$. Let $r$ be the radius of the disc that stands for the robot in the barrier
(by default the circumscribed radius of the costmap footprint) and $m$ a
safety margin. We
want a control policy that (G1)~makes monotone progress along $\sigma$ --
i.e., does not stall in a local minimum -- and (G2)~keeps the robot
clear of the obstacles in $\mathcal{O}$ through a barrier whose forward
invariance holds under stated conditions, while (G3)~running as a Nav2
controller at $\geq 10$~Hz on CPU.

The tension: G1 may require maneuvers that approach obstacles (to round a
trap), whereas a naive safety filter for G2 forbids exactly those
maneuvers. SE-MPPI's coordinator (Sec.~\ref{sec:coordination}) couples the
escape layer for G1 and the barrier for G2 through the barrier gain.

\section{Method: SE-MPPI}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes the controller that ships, tagged
\texttt{campaign2-controller}. The first version of the plugin differed in
four points that Sec.~\ref{sec:history} names; the escape described here
also contains four additions to the design we advised ourselves, which
Sec.~\ref{sec:extras} lists.

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

SE-MPPI subclasses the stock \texttt{MPPIController}, reusing its
optimizer for the nominal command, then post-processes that command each
cycle:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Entrapment detection} from progress along a per-task anchor
  path (single source of truth, shared with the critic).
\item \textbf{Escape}: while entrapped, the controller hands MPPI a short
  escape path to a waypoint in an opening, and an \texttt{EscapeCritic}
  adds a distance-field repulsion cost to the rollouts.
\item \textbf{Dynamic-obstacle tracking} from the local costmap.
\item \textbf{Coordination}: resolve the CBF gain $\alpha$ from
  entrapment and TTC.
\item \textbf{CBF safety filter}: project the nominal $(v,\omega)$ onto
  the CBF-safe set via a small QP.
\end{enumerate}

The escape and safety layers thus act at complementary points --
sampling-time shaping of the path and the costs, and output-time projection
-- unified by a shared entrapment signal and the coordinated gain.

\subsection{Entrapment detection (single source of truth)}
\label{sec:entrapment}

\textbf{Progress on an anchor path.} Progress is the arc length along a
per-task \emph{anchor path}, the first plan of the task. The nearest-index
progress of the first version, re-anchored on every replan, let one path
cell of motion along a fresh replan count as progress; Nav2's behavior tree
replans every second, so this ended every escape within seconds
(Sec.~\ref{sec:history}). A progress event now needs $0.10$~m of arc
length beyond the best so far. A replan to the same goal (same frame, and an endpoint within two
cells of the local costmap, $0.10$~m here, of the previous endpoint) is only a
\emph{candidate}: it becomes the anchor, and counts as an event, once the
signed advance along the chain of candidates reaches $0.5$~m. Entrapment is
declared when no event occurs for $W=30$ control cycles ($3$~s at $10$~Hz; in the live campaign
the loop was paced by the wall clock, Sec.~\ref{sec:campaign2})
and is suppressed within a goal tolerance of $0.5$~m. The window counts
control cycles, not seconds: at the $20$~Hz of the released parameter file the
same $30$ cycles are $1.5$~s. The movement watchdog ($3$~s) and the re-selection period
($15$~s) of Sec.~\ref{sec:escape} are also converted to cycles at the configured controller
frequency, so all three stretch in wall time when the loop misses its rate. The escape is decided
at the start of a cycle from the detector state at the end of the previous
cycle, a lag of one cycle. The controller publishes the detector state to the
\texttt{EscapeCritic} through a shared atomic flag in a registry keyed by the controller
name. The critic scores the rollouts before the detector of the current cycle runs, so the
critic and the escape path act on the previous cycle's state, while the coordinator uses the
state of the current cycle. Escape and safety therefore agree on whether the robot is
trapped up to one cycle: on the cycle in which entrapment begins or ends, the gain $\alpha$
already follows the new state and the repulsion and the escape path still follow the old
one. The mirror has the same split.

\textbf{No valid control.} When every rollout collides, the optimizer
throws \texttt{NoValidControl}. The controller catches it, runs the progress
update and the detector as on a normal cycle, and rethrows. This update
acts only if the controller server tolerates the failure. With Nav2's
default \texttt{failure\_tolerance} of $0$ the server rethrows, publishes
zero velocity and calls \texttt{reset()} on every controller, which clears
the detector in the same cycle; the live campaign of
Sec.~\ref{sec:campaign2} runs with this default, so the update did not act
there (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\textbf{Reverse speed.} The parameter file keeps \texttt{vx\_min} at
$-0.35$~m/s. With $\texttt{vx\_min}>0$ the robot cannot stop or back off, so
the escape cannot act; the controller logs a warning, and the campaign
driver refuses such a parameter file.

\subsection{Escape (detect-and-switch)}
\label{sec:escape}

While the robot is entrapped, the controller applies three rules. Table~\ref{tab:params} lists the values.

\textbf{(1) Opening selection.} The controller casts $36$ rays over the local costmap
out to a sensing horizon of $2.0$~m (or the costmap edge). Among the rays that reach
the horizon, whose free length (to the first cell with cost at least $253$, the inscribed
band, not the wall, or to the first unknown cell) is at least $0.6$~m, and that do not lead to an avoided
place (Sec.~\ref{sec:extras}), it takes the ray closest to the goal bearing. If there is
none, it takes the longest ray whose free length is at least $0.6$~m and that does not lead
to an avoided place. Both uses of the $0.6$~m threshold are part of addition (d)
(Sec.~\ref{sec:extras}); the advice had no threshold on the longest-ray fallback. If excluding rays leaves nothing, it repeats the choice without the avoid list.
A hysteresis of $0.5$~rad keeps the previous bearing unless a new ray is closer to the
goal bearing by more than that. The horizon test matters: from the mouth of a pocket the
ray back into the pocket is longer than the rays along the walls, so a plain
longest-ray rule re-enters the pocket.

\textbf{(2) Escape path.} The waypoint lies on the selected bearing at
$\min(c-r-0.05,\,2.5)$~m, with $c$ the free length of the ray and $r$ the radius of the
CBF disc (\texttt{se\_cbf\_robot\_radius}; its default $0$ takes the circumscribed radius that
the costmap reports for the footprint). The campaign file does not set it, and every live
launch logged \texttt{robot\_radius=0.234} at configure, for a footprint built from
\texttt{robot\_radius} $0.22$~m, so $r=0.234$~m live. A deployer who sets the CBF radius
therefore also moves the escape waypoint. The waypoint is taken only if that distance is at
least $0.2$~m. Four values are compiled constants, not YAML values: the floor of $0.2$~m, the
margin of $0.05$~m in the waypoint distance, the size $6$ of the failed list (below) and the
same-goal test of Sec.~\ref{sec:entrapment} (two costmap cells).
If no ray is usable when the escape starts, MPPI keeps the behavior-tree path; if none is usable at
a re-selection, MPPI keeps the previous escape path. In both cases the selection runs again in every
cycle, and the repulsion stays on while the robot is entrapped. The mirror does the same. The cap of
$2.5$~m is inactive while the sensing horizon $2.0$~m is at most $2.5+r+0.05=2.784$~m, as
here: the longest hop is $2.0-0.234-0.05=1.716$~m. The controller builds a
path from the robot to the waypoint and hands it to MPPI through
\texttt{MPPIController::setPlan}, before the optimizer call; the goal, alignment and
path-following critics then pull the rollouts toward the waypoint. The path of the
behavior tree stays in the controller for progress and for the near-goal test,
and MPPI receives it again when the escape ends. Nav2's controller server checks goal
arrival against its own copy of the behavior-tree path, so the substitution cannot fake
a goal. The path is published on a topic of its own for logging.

\textbf{(3) Hops.} The waypoint stays frozen until the robot is within $0.4$~m of it,
$15$~s have passed, the robot has moved less than $0.15$~m in $3$~s, or the straight line
to it is blocked. Each new selection is a hop. A waypoint that was not reached is
remembered as failed for the episode. As the code reads, the waypoint is appended to the
failed list in every cycle in which a re-selection fires, also when the selection is then
not taken (no usable ray, or a waypoint closer than $0.2$~m), so the six entries can hold
copies of one waypoint. The mirror has the same quirk. We did not remove the duplicates
and did not measure their effect; the campaign ran with them.

\textbf{End of the escape.} Entrapment, and with it the escape and its avoid lists, ends at
the first progress event ($0.10$~m beyond the best arc length on the anchor, or an adoption).
The controller then clears the escape state and hands MPPI the behavior-tree path again.
All escape memory (anchor path, detector, stall pose, avoid and failed lists, obstacle tracks)
lives for one task. A plan that arrives after the control loop has been idle for more than
\texttt{se\_task\_idle\_threshold} (compiled default $1.0$~s, not set in the campaign file)
starts a new task, and Jazzy's controller server calls \texttt{reset()} at the end of every
\texttt{FollowPath} goal; both clear this memory. Under a recovery tree, each recovery
therefore restarts the escape from scratch. Campaign 2 did not exercise this: no SE trial
invoked a recovery or had a progress failure.

\textbf{Repulsion cost.} The \texttt{EscapeCritic} keeps the distance-field repulsive cost of
the classical form \cite{khatib_apf},
$U=\tfrac12\eta\,(1/d - 1/d_0)^2$ for $d<d_0$, with $\eta=1$ (the compiled default
\texttt{apf\_eta}; the campaign file does not set it) and $d_0=0.6$~m
(\texttt{apf\_influence\_dist}). Here $d$ is the Dijkstra distance (8-connected grid) to the
nearest cell with cost at least $253$ (the inscribed band, which lies one robot radius from the
wall, not the wall; unknown space is not an obstacle), clamped below at half a cell
($0.025$~m). The cost of a rollout is the mean of $U$ over its steps that lie on the map
(steps off the map are skipped). It keeps rollouts off the trapping surface during the turn.
No weight multiplies it: \texttt{repulsion\_weight} ($2.0$ in the parameter file) is unused
while \texttt{use\_apf} is true (its default). The campaign parameter file is frozen, so the
comment that would say so is not added there; the released parameter file carries it. The critic's own
gap-bearing cost, which rewards rollout endpoints that head toward the
selected opening and is a form of the free-space subgoal and gap guidance of
GP-MPPI \cite{gp_mppi} and Potential Gap \cite{potential_gap}, is off in the shipped
configuration (\texttt{gap\_weight} $0$) and kept only for ablation. The first version
relied on that cost and selected a sideways opening instead of routing out of the pocket
(Sec.~\ref{sec:history}). Because the escape is active only when entrapped,
free-space behavior is unchanged (detect-and-switch, not always-on, as in DRPA-MPPI
\cite{drpa_mppi}).

\subsection{Additions to the advised design}
\label{sec:extras}

The design we advised ourselves before writing code contained the rules above except
four additions. We found (a) and (b) needed together for the development sweeps of a family
of pockets to pass (Sec.~\ref{sec:devtest}): the measured ablation below switches them off
jointly, and we ran no ablation of either one alone. That ablation is confounded with the
position of a geometry in the sweep process: run alone, geometries passed more often without
the additions than in the sweeps (below), so it does not show that (a) and (b) are necessary
in general. The reason given for each below rests on development
observations, not on a separate measurement. The floor (c) and the threshold (d) are safeguards for other
configurations; under the shipped parameters (c) never acts and (d) never acts on horizon
rays. We accepted all four as the shipped algorithm; every statement in this paper about
SE-MPPI is a statement about the controller with them.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] \textbf{Avoid lists.} The selection excludes rays that lead to the pose where
  entrapment was declared ($1.0$~m), to the origins of the last three hops, and to failed
  waypoints ($0.6$~m, at most six remembered). An avoided place counts only if it is
  ahead of the ray (less than $90^\circ$ off), farther from the robot than its radius, not
  more than that radius beyond the end of the ray, and the ray passes within that radius of
  it. From the mouth of a pocket the ray
  back to the stall pose reaches the horizon and is closest to the goal bearing;
  without the exclusion the robot re-enters. Without the hop origins a rotated pocket
  made the selection swing between two places.
\item[(b)] \textbf{Movement watchdog.} A re-selection when the robot has moved less than
  $0.15$~m in $3$~s. On one development geometry MPPI stood still in front of a gap that a
  binary cost threshold called free but whose inflation cost was about $160$; the advised
  $15$~s period is too slow for Nav2's $10$~s progress checker.
\item[(c)] \textbf{Waypoint floor.} A candidate waypoint closer than $0.2$~m is not taken.
  Inert here: a selected ray has a free length of at least $0.6$~m, so the waypoint is at
  least $0.6-0.234-0.05=0.316$~m away.
\item[(d)] \textbf{Clearance threshold.} The $0.6$~m threshold on the free length of a ray
  applies to the horizon candidates and to the longest-ray fallback; the advice had no
  threshold on the fallback. On horizon rays it is inert here: the local costmap is $3$~m
  wide and re-centres on the robot at its $5$~Hz update, so a ray that reaches the horizon or
  the costmap edge is at least about $1.4$~m long (shortest logged live: $1.42$~m). On the
  fallback it excludes rays shorter than $0.6$~m. The fallback never fired live: all 188
  hops logged in campaign 2 (evidence trials included) took a horizon ray, so (d) did not act in
  any live trial. The mirror's switch for the additions removes
  the threshold from both places. Restoring it on the fallback in the advised arm changed no
  outcome on the U-trap family ($29$ of $50$, no seed differs;
  \texttt{experiments/results\_2d\_v2\_advised/}\allowbreak\texttt{FALLBACK\_THRESHOLD.md}).
\end{enumerate}

parameter, so the ablation leaves them on; (c) does not act under these parameters, and (d)
acts only on the fallback. The test is the headless sweep of
Sec.~\ref{sec:devtest} over the 56 valid development geometries, one run each. MPPI's

\subsection{CBF safety filter (dynamic obstacles)}
\label{sec:cbf}
To make the barrier relative-degree one in $(v,\omega)$ for a unicycle,
safety is enforced on a \textbf{look-ahead point}
$P = (x,y) + L\,(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, which is fully actuated by
$(v,\omega)$ through the Jacobian
\begin{equation}
G=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & -L\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & L\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For each obstacle $o$ with $d=P-p_o$ and effective radius
$R = r + R_o + m$:
\begin{equation}
h_o = \lVert d\rVert^2 - R^2, \qquad \dot h_o = 2\,d^\top (G\,u - v_o).
\end{equation}
The continuous-time CBF condition $\dot h_o + \alpha\,h_o \ge 0$
\cite{ames_cbf_tac,ames_cbf_ecc}, enforced once per control cycle, is
linear in $u$. We solve a small QP (OSQP) \cite{osqp} that minimizes
\begin{equation}
\lVert u - u_\text{nom}\rVert^2 + \rho\,\delta^2
\end{equation}
subject to the per-obstacle CBF rows (relaxed by a single slack
$\delta\ge0$ for feasibility) and the input box limits. Obstacles are
pruned to the nearest $20$ (a compiled cap) by \emph{clearance} (surface gap), not center
distance. If the QP can only stay safe by relaxing the barrier
($\delta>\epsilon$) or fails, the controller sets the linear velocity to zero
(forward or reverse; the controller's log calls it the forward velocity) while keeping the QP's turn (or, on solver failure, the
box-clamped nominal turn) -- stop rather than drive
into an imminent collision. Static structure is intentionally
\textbf{excluded} from the CBF (handled by the MPPI obstacle critic and
costmap inflation); only obstacles that are moving and small enough to be
a movable body are admitted (a track must pass the occupancy-persistence veto
\texttt{is\_dynamic}, move at least $0.1$~m/s and have a radius of at most $1.0$~m),
which we found essential in a real costmap
(Sec.~\ref{sec:integration}).

Because this condition is checked once per control cycle (a sampled-data
CBF), strict between-sample forward invariance would require a
sampled-data margin tightening $R$ by an $O(\dot h_{\max}\Delta t)$ term;
in practice the obstacle-radius margin $m$ absorbs this gap, and we report
the empirical safety rate rather than claim continuous-time invariance
between samples.

\subsection{Escape--safety coordination: gain scheduling under a conditional invariance lemma}
\label{sec:coordination}

The coordinator resolves the gain:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:alpha}
\alpha = \begin{cases}
\alpha_\text{base} & \text{not entrapped, or TTC} < \tau \text{ (imminent)}\\
\alpha_\text{escape} & \text{entrapped and TTC} \ge \tau
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_\text{escape} > \alpha_\text{base} > 0$, where TTC is the
minimum time-to-collision over tracked obstacles under the current
command.\footnote{The released controller's coordinator additionally holds
$\alpha$ at $\alpha_\text{base}$ whenever the online conformal bound $q$ on
obstacle-prediction error exceeds a trust threshold ($0.25$~m by default;
the calibrator is enabled by default), and that bound inflates the
effective radius $R$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:cbf} -- the SE-Predict margin noted
in Sec.~\ref{sec:related}, not claimed by this paper. An implementation
following Eq.~(\ref{eq:alpha}) alone therefore reproduces the benchmark
configurations of Sec.~\ref{sec:bench2d}, not the released defaults.} Raising $\alpha$ relaxes the constraint $\dot h \ge -\alpha h$ --
permitting the robot to approach an obstacle faster, as an escape
maneuver requires -- while the TTC override snaps the gain back to
$\alpha_\text{base}$ (safety first) when a dynamic obstacle is imminent.
The next result states that, under explicit conditions, this schedule
does not by itself give up forward invariance. It is a known property of
CBFs with time-varying class-$\mathcal{K}$ terms and follows the same
argument as the relative-degree-one case of the invariance result for
adaptive CBFs with time-varying penalty functions \cite[Thm.~3]{ada_cbf},
which is stated there for Lipschitz-continuous controllers, whereas our
switched gain yields a discontinuous command; related time-varying and
optimized decay rates appear in \cite{optdecay_cbf} and \cite{rt_cbf}. We
restate it with the standard integrating-factor proof for completeness
and claim no new mathematics.

\noindent\textbf{Proposition (invariance under a time-varying gain; after
\cite{ada_cbf}).}
\textit{Fix an obstacle $j$, suppose the safety-filter QP is feasible
without slack throughout the interval considered (the shared
$\delta(t)=0$), and assume the continuous-time inequality
$\dot h_j(t) \ge -\alpha(t)\,h_j(t)$ holds for almost every $t\ge0$ --
an assumption between samples, since discrete-instant QP feasibility does
not by itself imply the intersample inequality (the sampled-data caveat of
Sec.~\ref{sec:cbf}). Let $\alpha:[0,\infty)\to[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]
\subset(0,\infty)$ be \emph{any} measurable, positive, bounded gain
schedule -- in particular the coordinator's piecewise-constant switching
between $\alpha_\text{base}$ and $\alpha_\text{escape}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:alpha})
and its TTC override. If $h_j(0)\ge0$, then
$h_j(t)\ge0$ for all $t\ge0$ -- the look-ahead point $P$ does not enter
obstacle $j$'s inflated disc, as modeled (body safety approximate; see
Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).}

\noindent\textbf{Proof.} Define the integrating factor
$\varphi(t)=\exp\!\big(\int_0^t \alpha(s)\,ds\big)$, finite and strictly
positive because $\alpha\le\alpha_{\max}$. Then, almost everywhere,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}\big[\varphi(t)\,h_j(t)\big]
= \varphi(t)\big[\dot h_j(t)+\alpha(t)\,h_j(t)\big] \ \ge\ 0,
\end{equation}
so $\varphi(t)\,h_j(t)$ is non-decreasing, hence
$\varphi(t)\,h_j(t)\ge\varphi(0)\,h_j(0)=h_j(0)\ge0$. Since
$\varphi(t)>0$, $h_j(t)\ge0$ for all $t$. $\hfill\blacksquare$

Positivity and boundedness are stronger than the argument needs: a
nonnegative, locally integrable $\alpha$ already makes $\varphi$ finite
and positive. We keep the stronger hypothesis because it matches the
schedule of Eq.~(\ref{eq:alpha}).

\noindent\textbf{Corollary.} Under the slack-free and between-sample
conditions of the Proposition, forward invariance of
$\mathcal{C}_j=\{h_j\ge0\}$ holds for \emph{every} positive, bounded gain
schedule -- in particular for the coordinator's switching between
$\alpha_\text{base}$ and $\alpha_\text{escape}$, and for the TTC override
that drops $\alpha$ back to $\alpha_\text{base}$. If these conditions hold
for every row, $\mathcal{C}=\bigcap_j\mathcal{C}_j$ is invariant; the
argument is per-obstacle and gain-agnostic, so sharing a single
$\alpha(t)$ across rows is immaterial to the invariance statement itself.

Under these conditions, raising $\alpha$ therefore does not permit
$h_j<0$; it only weakens the lower bound on $\dot h_j$, letting the robot
approach $\partial\mathcal{C}_j$ faster (bolder detours) inside the same
set. This is the design rationale for coordinating rather than stacking
the two layers: in this idealized setting, the gain trades caution for
maneuverability without giving up invariance. The guarantee is narrow. It
concerns the look-ahead point, not the robot body; it covers only the
tracked dynamic obstacles under the constant-velocity model, not static
structure; it applies to an obstacle only while that obstacle stays in the
tracked, unpruned set $\mathcal{O}$ (an obstacle that slows below the
speed threshold for dynamic obstacles, merges into a cluster too large to
be a movable body, or is pruned beyond the nearest few by clearance leaves
the QP); it assumes the continuous-time inequality between samples; and it
requires $\delta=0$, so it does not cover any cycle in which the QP uses
slack. Because the slack is shared, one imminent obstacle can momentarily
loosen the margin on every other obstacle in the same QP solve, so we
report slack usage empirically: in the 2D benchmark of
Sec.~\ref{sec:bench2d} the QP never engages slack on the mover-free
families, and uses nonzero slack in $26$--$35$ of $50$ narrow-dynamic
trials (max $0.47$) and $47$--$48$ of $50$ dynamic-family trials (max
$0.014$) per CBF-carrying config (Sec.~\ref{sec:evsf}). In cycles with
$\delta>\epsilon$ the brake of Sec.~\ref{sec:cbf} sets the linear velocity of the QP command to zero,
and bounded-prediction-error robustness is the subject of follow-up work
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Dynamic-obstacle tracking}
\label{sec:tracking}

The tracker clusters costmap cells at or above the inscribed-obstacle cost ($253$) and
matches clusters to persistent tracks greedily, nearest track first, in cluster order; each
track is matched to at most one current cluster within a gate, so two clusters cannot inherit
one track's velocity, and a track survives up to $3$ unmatched frames
(\texttt{se\_track\_max\_missed}). A split or merge can still shift the centroid of the matched
cluster and so produce a phantom velocity (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2results}). The tracker estimates a
clamped constant velocity. The controller stamps each costmap frame with the node clock in
the control cycle that first sees a changed grid, not with the costmap's update time; with the
local costmap at $5$~Hz and the controller at $10$~Hz in the campaign, the time step of the
velocity estimate is rounded to control cycles, and on a robot the costmap latency adds to this
jitter. Its output $\{p_o, R_o, v_o\}$ feeds the
coordinator (TTC) and the CBF filter. The constant-velocity model is a
deliberate, replaceable baseline (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\textbf{Configuration that ran live.} The campaign parameter file does not set
\texttt{se\_classify\_static}, \texttt{se\_predict\_horizon} or \texttt{se\_conformal}. Their
code defaults are true, so every live SE trial ran with the occupancy-based static/dynamic
classification (the \texttt{is\_dynamic} veto above), the horizon prediction and the online
conformal margin on. The 2D mirror has none of the three. The statements of this paper about the
tracker and the CBF filter hold for this configuration only. A cell counts as static only after
\texttt{se\_static\_min\_frames} ($10$, the code default, not set in the campaign file) tracker frames
of occupancy, so newly seen structure, such as a wall entering the rolling window, is a dynamic
candidate during that period. We state this as a code fact; we did not establish that it caused the
phantom admissions of Sec.~\ref{sec:c2results}.

\section{Implementation (Nav2-Native)}
\label{sec:implementation}

SE-MPPI is built on ROS~2 Jazzy + Nav2 \cite{nav2}. The package
\texttt{nav2\_se\_controller} ships four shared libraries and one executable: a plugin-free
\texttt{se\_mppi\_core} (CBF filter, coordinators, clustering, static filter, predictor,
tracker, path progress, opening search; the entrapment detector is header-only), the
\texttt{escape\_critic} MPPI critic (with the repulsion and the critic's gap search), the
\texttt{safe\_escape\_controller}, and \texttt{mppi\_rollout\_check} with the
\texttt{check\_mppi\_rollout} executable. Separating the plugin-free core from
the two plugin libraries resolves a \texttt{class\_loader} factory
conflict that otherwise prevents the critic and controller from
co-loading.

Two integration details proved essential and are easy to get wrong:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Critic namespace.} Nav2's \texttt{CriticManager} resolves a
  critics-list entry \texttt{NAME} to the plugin class
  \texttt{mppi::critics::NAME}. A critic in any other namespace can be
  unit-tested via pluginlib directly but will \textbf{never} load from
  the critics list. \texttt{EscapeCritic} therefore lives in
  \texttt{mppi::critics}.
\item \textbf{Jazzy critic data uses xtensor}, not the Eigen of the
  \texttt{main} branch; the critic was written against the installed
  headers.
\end{itemize}

The QP uses \texttt{osqp-eigen} \cite{osqp}. The build is reproducible via
RoboStack (conda-forge) so the build does not depend on the host OS version
(validated on Ubuntu 20.04 with an NVIDIA GPU). The package's modules for this paper (entrapment detector, CBF filter,
coordinator, tracker, gap search, repulsion, path progress, and plugin
loading) carry \textbf{44 unit tests} (gtest), plus linters; the wider package
additionally carries tests for the follow-up papers' prediction stack
(static/dynamic occupancy classification, trajectory prediction, conformal
calibration) and multi-robot coordination module (SE-Predict,
Multi-SE-MPPI), plus the live-injection ABI regression of
Sec.~\ref{sec:integration}, bringing the algorithm test suite
(\texttt{test/test\_*.cpp}) to 82 \texttt{TEST} cases across 13 files. The
deployment tests in \texttt{test/deployment} (the controller on a lifecycle
node inside the stock MPPI optimizer, the escape rules, the path-progress
tracker, the rollout-direction guard and the headless closed-loop test)
add 148 \texttt{TEST} cases in 10 files. The code evaluated in the headless
sweeps and in the live campaign is the tag \texttt{campaign2-controller}; the
mirror re-run and the design-stage benchmark of Appendix~\ref{app:v1} ran at
the commits named in Appendix~\ref{sec:repro}.

\subsection{Live history: campaign 1 and the four defects}
\label{sec:history}

An earlier version of this paper reported live runs from July 2026 in which
no run reached its goal, and attributed that to the host (a real-time factor of
$0.3$ to $0.6$ and phantom costmap cells from transform smear). We withdraw
that attribution. A pre-registered live re-run (campaign 1) drove the same
U-pocket testbed in 48 registered trials at a real-time factor between
$0.93$ and $0.97$: 4 on an affected build and 44 valid trials on the
correct build. None reached the goal: the 44 valid trials ended in
42 timeouts and 2 aborted goals, 13 of them with an escape episode. The
raw files, the analysis and the root-cause note are in
\texttt{experiments/results\_pilot\_rerun/}. The note names four defects of the
controller and its parameter file, which act in this order.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Forward-only speed range.} The pilot's \texttt{vx\_min} was
  $0.1$, so the robot could not stop or back off. In the 20 valid trials with
  that setting it crept into the back wall; the chassis touched the wall in 14 of them. Once all
  rollouts collide the optimizer throws \texttt{NoValidControl} every cycle.
\item \textbf{Detector skipped on a throw.} The first version updated its
  entrapment detector only after the optimizer call returned, so it never
  saw a stall that ended in a throw.
\item \textbf{Progress re-anchored on every replan.} A replan to the same
  goal re-anchored the furthest-reached path index, so one cell of motion
  (2.5~cm) along the new path counted as progress. All 70 closed escape
  episodes of the parameter set that allowed reversing ended at path index 1 or 2,
  at a median of 3.1~s.
\item \textbf{Escape did not route out.} In that parameter set, seven episodes
  of 24 to 83~s occurred in 5 of the 6 SE-MPPI trials, and the robot stayed within
  0.34~m of its stopping point. The gap selection was not logged. From
  the pocket geometry and the observed heading we infer that it chose a
  sideways opening and rocked in place; this inference is not a measurement,
  and the repaired controller logs every selection.
\end{enumerate}

None of the four exists in the Python 2D mirror of the first version, which used
distance-to-goal progress, a clearance of $2.4$~m on a $3.0$~m window and a subgoal that
replaced the goal. The benchmark result of the first version, kept in
Appendix~\ref{app:v1}, therefore described an idealized design and not the plugin.

\textbf{A defective distributed binary.} Some distributed builds of
\texttt{nav2\_mppi\_controller} negate cosine and sine in the batch
rollout integration for part of the heading range, so the optimizer scores its
samples at mirrored positions; a controller built on them commands motion away
from the goal for some headings. The upstream report
(RoboStack/ros-jazzy\#290) names the interaction of fast-math with xsimd's
use of signed zeros as the suspected cause and was closed on 2026-10-10. The
plugin carries a check, \texttt{check\_mppi\_rollout}, that compares $24{,}576$ rollout points of
the library it loads with the closed form at a tolerance of 1~mm, and
\texttt{SafeEscapeController} refuses to configure on a library that fails
it. The check guards only \texttt{SafeEscapeController}: any other controller that loads the
same library is unguarded. Run \texttt{check\_mppi\_rollout} on every target install (the apt
binaries are untested), and never set \texttt{se\_enforce\_rollout\_check} to false on a
moving robot. The July 2026 runs used an affected build; whether that decided
their outcome is not established. We did not use any result obtained on an
affected build in the claims below.

\subsection{Deployment faults}
\label{sec:integration}

Three integration faults surfaced in the live stack that the in-process unit tests did not
catch, each reported as a deployment lesson:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Static walls in the CBF.} Feeding every inscribed-cost cluster
  (including static walls) into the CBF makes the barrier infeasible
  everywhere and freezes the robot. Scoping the CBF to clusters that the tracker
  classifies as dynamic (Sec.~\ref{sec:cbf}) stopped the wall freeze; it did not stop
  phantom admissions, which occurred in most live SE-MPPI trials (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2results}).
\item \textbf{Compute budget.} The per-cycle budget must be matched to the
  host (horizon preserved, rate and batch reduced). The comment in the campaign
  parameter file records what happened on this host otherwise: the $20$~Hz, $56$-step,
  batch-$2000$ configuration ran at about $5$~Hz, stalled the controller server's executor
  past the $4$~s lifecycle bond, and the lifecycle manager tore the whole stack down. With the
  stock bringup the bond timeout cannot be set from the parameter file. The released defaults
  ($20$~Hz, $56$ steps, batch $1000$) were not evaluated. Deployment warning: measure the
  achieved controller rate on the target (Nav2 warns when the controller misses its desired
  rate) and expect a lifecycle teardown when the executor starves.
\item \textbf{xtensor ABI mismatch.} The installed
  \texttt{libmppi\_controller.so} is compiled with
  \texttt{XTENSOR\_USE\_XSIMD}~+~AVX2 (an \texttt{xsimd::aligned\_allocator}
  element type), whereas our plugin compiled xtensor with the default
  \texttt{std::allocator}. Because \texttt{CriticData}'s tensors cross the
  pluginlib boundary by reference, the first \texttt{EscapeCritic} write to
  \texttt{data.costs} was an ODR violation that segfaulted the entire Nav2
  process on the first live escape injection (3/3 reproducible). The
  in-process unit tests never caught it, because they compile every
  translation unit with one consistent flag set. Fixed by compiling the
  critic against the installed headers' flags (per-target, since a global
  AVX2 flip breaks the separately built OSQP--Eigen boundary); a regression
  test replays the injection path with live tensor shapes
  (\texttt{test\_escape\_injection\_live\_shapes.cpp}), and
  \texttt{scripts/check\_simd\_abi.py} checks the storage type in CI.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Headless closed-loop development test}
\label{sec:devtest}

Before any further live trial we built a headless closed-loop test that would have
caught defects 2 to 4. It runs the real optimizer (batch 600, 28 steps, $dt=0.1$) and
the controller on a lifecycle node with a drawn costmap, integrates the commands on a
unicycle at the control period, replans a straight path from the robot every
$1.0$~s as the behavior tree does, and emulates the controller server by catching
\texttt{NoValidControl} and applying zero velocity. It is the development loop for the
escape.

\textbf{Robot radius in the rig.} The controller in the rig took its robot radius,
$0.114$~m (logged as \texttt{robot\_radius=0.114} in every controller start line of the
confirmation sweep logs, which lie outside the repository; per-log counts in \texttt{experiments/campaign2/sweeps/}\allowbreak\texttt{robot\_radius\_lines.txt}),
from the default footprint of \texttt{Costmap2DROS}, which the rig does not set. The rig's drawn
costmap and its contact model use $0.22$~m; the live controller used $0.234$~m
(Sec.~\ref{sec:escape}). The radius enters the waypoint distance and the
CBF disc, so for the same ray the waypoint of the rig lies $0.12$~m farther out than the live
one. We did not change the rig.

\textbf{Family rule.} The test pockets are generated from a written rule, fixed in a
committed file before the first run
(\texttt{test/deployment/UTRAP\_DEV\_GEOMETRIES.md}). A pocket has width $w\in\{1.3,
1.6, 2.2, 2.6\}$~m, depth $d\in\{1.1, 1.5, 1.8\}$~m, rotation $\theta\in\{-25, 0, 15,
30\}^\circ$ and symmetric or asymmetric side walls (top wall at $0.6$ of the
bottom wall): $4\times3\times4\times2=96$ combinations. A combination is valid only if the straight
start--goal path enters the mouth, start and goal are at least $0.8$~m from every wall,
the straight line is blocked, and the pocket is not the live development scenario. The generator
finds 56 valid geometries. Two primary geometries, outside the grid,
gate the test (\texttt{w22\_d13\_r30\_a10} and \texttt{w16\_d15\_r-15\_a6}). A run
passes if the robot enters the pocket, leaves it within $60$~s, reaches within $0.5$~m
of the goal within $150$~s, and has no wall contact and no \texttt{NoValidControl}
throw. The development scenario \texttt{s1\_utrap} is excluded from the family, and a
guard test fails if the name of a held-out world appears in the repository outside
the campaign directory.

\textbf{Result and its status.} The shipped controller passes 55 or 56 of the 56 valid
geometries per sweep, and without additions (a) and (b) 14 to 18 in the three archived
confirmation sweeps (19 in an earlier, unarchived sweep; Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
The additions were worked on until this family passed, so the family is a development
set, not a test set. In addition to the family, the additions were developed on static gap tests at the stall
pose of the live development scenario \texttt{s1\_utrap}; that is a use of a live scenario in the design
of a parameter, and the reason \texttt{s1\_utrap} is a development scenario and not a test.

\textbf{The twin of s3.} The held-out campaign pocket \texttt{s3\_ctrap} was specified before
the tuning and authored by a separately prompted coding assistant from the geometry alone; it was not driven live and not
run headless. It is not outside the family: reflected about $y=0$ it matches the family member
\texttt{w22\_d15\_r-25\_a6} to within $0.1$~m in width, $0.05$~m in depth and $0.09$ in the
wall ratio. The additions were worked on until that twin passed in the sweeps; run alone
with the additions off, the twin also passed 3 of 3 (\texttt{PREREG.md}), so it is not
evidence that the additions were needed for it. We therefore do not use
\texttt{s3} as the test of transfer; Sec.~\ref{sec:campaign2} uses a second held-out pocket
that lies outside the family.

\subsection{Randomized benchmark on a Python 2D mirror}
\label{sec:bench2d}

\textbf{Every quantitative result in this section comes from a Python 2D
mirror, not from the Nav2 controller.} The mirror re-implements the
controller's primitives in NumPy and OSQP \cite{osqp}: the MPPI sampler, a repulsion of
the same form with other parameters (Appendix), the ray cast, the look-ahead-point CBF-QP, the $\alpha$ coordination with TTC
override, and, in the version used here, the shipped escape: progress on an anchor path
with simulated one-second straight replans, the horizon-ray selection with hysteresis,
the frozen-waypoint hops, the one-cycle lag, and a zero-command mode when every
rollout collides. The mirror keeps running after such a cycle, as a controller server with a
failure tolerance of $-1$ would, not as the campaign's tolerance of $0$; in the shipped arm one
F trial on \texttt{dynamic} (a success) and one on \texttt{narrowdyn} (a collision) passed
through such cycles, and no \texttt{utrap} trial did. A flag switches the four additions of Sec.~\ref{sec:extras} on
(the shipped algorithm, variant \texttt{v2}) or off (the design as advised, variant
\texttt{v2\_advised}). A test calls the C++ selection, ray cast, progress chain and detector through a shim
and compares them with the mirror; the hop state machine and the one-cycle lag were
checked by reading only. The mirror still differs from the controller in the ways
the Appendix lists: it computes geometry analytically against circular obstacles, gives
the CBF and the TTC the true obstacle states, and uses its own cost. It does not run
Nav2's progress checker or behavior tree, so it is a controller-level benchmark. We
re-ran the full benchmark, with the same families, seeds, configs and budget as the
design-stage benchmark of Appendix~\ref{app:v1}, on this mirror, so that the paper's
numbers describe the algorithm that ships.

\textbf{Setup.} Four seed-deterministic scenario families exercise the two
weaknesses and their conjunction: \texttt{utrap} (a finite U/C pocket with
the goal behind the back wall -- a static local minimum), \texttt{clutter}
(a random field of 5--9 circular obstacles with a guaranteed
footprint-clear path), \texttt{dynamic} (1--2 pedestrian-like crossing
movers timed to reach the robot's path), and \texttt{narrowdyn} (the
\texttt{utrap} pocket \emph{plus} movers sweeping the pocket mouth during
the escape window). Six configs toggle the mechanisms:
\textbf{A}~stock (no escape/CBF), \textbf{C}~escape only, \textbf{D}~CBF
only, \textbf{E}~escape+CBF with
$\alpha_\text{escape}\!=\!\alpha_\text{base}$ (independent), \textbf{F}~full
SE-MPPI ($\alpha\!:\!2\!\to\!6$~+~TTC override, coordinated), and
\textbf{F$^{-}$} (F with the gap search off). C and F$^{-}$ exist only in the mirror: the
Nav2 overlays of the same names in \texttt{experiments/configs/ablations.yaml}
(\texttt{C\_escape\_detect}, \texttt{F\_minus\_gap}) do not reproduce them under the shipped
controller, which clears the shared entrapment flag while \texttt{se\_enabled} is false and
keeps its own opening search when the critic's gap search is off; no Nav2 result for them
is reported. Config~A is stock MPPI
without Nav2's progress checker and behavior-tree recoveries
\cite{nav2_progress,nav2_bt}, so it is a controller-level baseline, not
the full Nav2 stack. Each family is a pure function of an integer seed, so every
config is evaluated on an \emph{identical} scenario set, as the paired McNemar
design requires: $4~\text{families}\times50~\text{seeds}\times6~\text{configs}=1{,}200$
paired trials per arm, 40~s sim budget each; these are $200$ seeded worlds,
each run under all six configs, so the trials are paired rather than independent.
Success carries a Wilson 95\% CI \cite{wilson_ci}; the primary test is McNemar's
\cite{mcnemar} on paired outcomes; continuous metrics use the Mann--Whitney $U$
test \cite{mann_whitney} with Cliff's $\delta$ \cite{cliff_delta}, an unpaired
comparison that ignores the pairing, with time-to-goal and path length taken over
successful trials only, so we read these as descriptive; all $p$-values are
Holm--Bonferroni-corrected \cite{holm} within each family. The two arms
(shipped \texttt{v2}, advised \texttt{v2\_advised}) are 1{,}200 trials each, run at
the commit named in the Appendix, with 6 workers.

\subsubsection{Escape--safety coordination is null in this regime}
\label{sec:evsf}

The load-bearing contrast for the C3 contribution is \textbf{E
(independent) vs.\ F (coordinated)}: identical mechanisms differing only in
whether the CBF gain is coordinated with the escape phase. \emph{Across all
200 paired scenarios this contrast is null} (Table~\ref{tab:bench2d-stats},
Fig.~\ref{fig:e_vs_f}): the discordant counts are $0/0$ in every family,
McNemar $p=1$, and every continuous effect size is $|\delta|\le0.002$. The same
holds in the advised arm ($0/0$ in every family, $|\delta|\le0.005$). The gain does engage as designed
($\alpha_\text{max}=6$ during escape, TTC override active). The committed
per-trial slack telemetry (\texttt{slack\_max} in \texttt{trials.csv};
per-config rates in \texttt{stats.json} and \texttt{tables.md}) locates
where the barrier actually works. On the mover-free families
(\texttt{utrap}, \texttt{clutter}) the dynamic-obstacle CBF sees no
obstacles at all: slack is zero in every trial, E and F issue identical
commands, and the null there is structural. On \texttt{narrowdyn} the
barrier \emph{does} engage and even relaxes -- the QP uses nonzero slack in
$35/50$ trials for E and F ($26/50$ for D and F$^{-}$; max slack $0.47$) --
and on \texttt{dynamic} in $47$--$48/50$ trials (max $0.014$), yet
the E-vs-F contrast stays null. The correct reading is that
\textbf{the outcomes are insensitive to the $\alpha$ schedule}: where the
barrier is quiescent the two configs coincide exactly, and where it is active
the base and escape gains admit escape maneuvers whose outcomes are
indistinguishable at $N=50$. We report this honestly: \textbf{in this 2D
benchmark the coordination produces no measurable outcome difference over
independent escape+CBF.} The result is consistent with, and delimits, the
Proposition of Sec.~\ref{sec:coordination}: coordination is a \emph{mechanism
with a conditional invariance property}, not, in these scenarios, an outcome
improvement. An outcome-level benefit would require a regime where the choice
of $\alpha$ changes which escape maneuvers are admitted at outcome-deciding
moments -- faster or persistent movers, tighter margins, a dynamic gap the
escape route must thread while the barrier is live; this generator's
pedestrian-like movers produce barrier engagement but not that sensitivity.
The live campaign cannot test it either, because its pockets contain no dynamic
obstacle. Constructing and measuring that regime is future work
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}); we claim no coordination benefit we have not measured.

\subsection{Pre-registered live campaign 2}
\label{sec:campaign2}

\textbf{Question.} Does the repaired controller escape a pocket live, in a full ROS~2
Jazzy + Nav2 + Gazebo stack? The pre-registration (\texttt{experiments/campaign2/PREREG.md})
was frozen on 2026-10-18, before any counted trial, with the controller, the parameter
file, the worlds, the plan and the analysis code listed with their sha256 in a manifest
and marked by the local tag \texttt{campaign2-controller}. Every trial records the commit,
the build stamp, the hashes and the installed libraries; the driver refuses a trial
whose installed files differ from the frozen ones.

\textbf{Design.} The testbed is a walled arena. The start is $(-3,0)$ and the goal
$(3,0)$ in every scenario. The localization map is an empty box, so the pocket is absent
from the planner's map: the global planner draws a straight path into the pocket, and
only the controller can get the robot out. Four scenarios:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{s1\_utrap}: a pocket $1.7$~m wide and $1.85$~m deep; the development
  scenario.
\item \texttt{s3\_ctrap}: a three-wall pocket with an oblique back wall, $2.1$~m wide
  and $1.45$~m deep, rotated $25^\circ$. Held out in a limited sense: not driven live, not run headless,
  authored by a separately prompted coding assistant that saw only the geometric
  specification, but it has a near mirror twin in the
  development family (Sec.~\ref{sec:devtest}). Reported, not decision-bearing.
\item \texttt{s4\_ltrap}: an L-shaped dead end (Fig.~\ref{fig:s4}). The author specified it
  before anything was run on it; a separately prompted coding assistant that saw only the
  geometric specification, and not the controller, the family rule, the tuning or the
  campaign-1 analysis, wrote the world. The specifier (the author) knew the design, so only
  the world file was authored blind. Its corridor is $2.05$~m deep against $1.85$~m for
  \texttt{s1\_utrap}, the deepest development pocket: $0.20$~m, 11 percent deeper, and
  it has a closed bent leg, a shape the family does not contain. It lies inside the family's
  span in width ($1.3$~m) and rotation ($-15^\circ$). It also fails the family's rule V1
  (the straight line enters the corridor with a margin of $0.10$~m, not $0.3$~m). It carries the claim of transfer.
\item \texttt{s0\_open}: the testbed without the pocket; a control for false escapes and for
  the health of the stack.
\end{itemize}
Four configurations cross the controller with the behavior tree: C1, stock MPPI with
Nav2's default recovery tree; C2, SE-MPPI with that tree; C3, stock MPPI with a
replanning-only tree in which the first \texttt{FollowPath} failure ends the goal and
the progress-checker allowance is $30$~s; C4, SE-MPPI with that tree. Stock MPPI in C1 and C3 is the \texttt{SafeEscapeController}
plugin with \texttt{se\_enabled} false and without \texttt{EscapeCritic} in the critics
list: it passes the MPPI command through unchanged and
still enforces the rollout check; it is not a separate stock plugin. The parameter file is
the campaign-1 file with reversing allowed (\texttt{vx\_min} $-0.35$), Nav2's default
\texttt{failure\_tolerance} of $0$ and progress checker ($0.5$~m in $10$~s), and the MPPI
block unchanged ($10$~Hz, $28$ steps, $dt=0.1$, batch $1500$, inflation $0.45$~m). C1 and
C2 run on s0, s1 and s3; C3 and C4 on all four; six repetitions per cell. Trials ran in a
pre-registered fixed order, not a random one: the cell order rotates with the repetition
(\texttt{plan.py}; one place per repetition in Phase A, two in Phase B). In Phase A the order on s1
was C4, C3; C3, C4; C4, C3 for repetitions 0 to 2, then C4 alone for repetitions 3 to 5. The plan has
88 trials: Phase A (the gate: C4 on s1 six times and C3 on s1 three times, 9 trials) and Phase B (79
trials, which include 4 uncounted evidence trials and the 12 trials on s4). The driver refuses
Phase B and every trial on s3 and s4 until Phase A is complete and its gate has not fired. If
C4 on s1 reaches the goal in 1 of 6 or fewer, the campaign stops. A trial starts only when the
one-minute load average is below $2.0$ at two checks $30$~s apart and the busy CPU over those
$30$~s is below $0.5$ core, and it is valid if the foreign CPU averages at most $1.0$ core and
stays at most $3.0$ cores in every $30$~s window.

\textbf{Host and tolerances.} Every trial ran on one workstation, under WSL2, with an AMD
Ryzen 5 5500GT (12 threads) and simulated time (\texttt{use\_sim\_time}). The control loop was
paced by the wall clock, not by simulated time: over the 46 SE-MPPI trials the controller ran $9.83$
to $10.00$ cycles per wall-clock second and $10.14$ to $10.42$ per simulated second (post hoc,
\texttt{CBF\_ADMISSIONS.md}). The timers that count cycles (the stall window, the watchdog and the
re-selection period) were therefore wall-clock durations, a few percent
shorter in simulated time. On a robot the two clocks coincide. Over all 88 trials the stack used
$3.2$ to $4.2$ cores on average per trial, the peak one-minute load average was $1.79$ to $8.35$,
and the lowest real-time factor over a $10$~s window was $0.882$ ($0.944$ in the SE-MPPI pocket
trials); only the 12 C1 pocket trials logged missed control cycles ($2$ to $5$ each). The parameter
file loosens tolerances for the lag of the simulated transform tree: \texttt{transform\_tolerance}
$0.5$~s on the controller, the costmaps, the behavior server and the collision monitor ($1.0$~s
for AMCL). It sets \texttt{source\_timeout} of the collision monitor to $5.0$~s for a different
reason, which its comment records: on this host the scan lagged simulated time by more than $1$~s
under load, and at $1.0$~s the monitor declared the source invalid and set \texttt{cmd\_vel} to zero
in the middle of an escape. Campaign 2 did not measure scan age, which is also the input of the
costmaps and the tracker. The collision monitor stayed in the command chain
(\texttt{cmd\_vel\_smoothed} to \texttt{cmd\_vel}); its log in the 88 trials holds no line above
INFO level (\texttt{CBF\_ADMISSIONS.md}). Do not copy these values to a robot, and measure the
sensor latency on the target. The only \texttt{collision\_monitor} polygon is disabled
(\texttt{enabled: False}), so the live results had no active \texttt{collision\_monitor}
zone. It was disabled in the earlier smoke tests because its approach polygon
(\texttt{time\_before\_collision} $1.2$~s) slowed the robot almost to a stop in the narrow
testbed rooms (\texttt{RUN.md}). No escape, live or headless, ran with an active approach
polygon. A deployer who enables it, as we advise, must test escapes again, because the monitor
may slow or stop the turns in a pocket. The MPPI cost critic ran with
\texttt{consider\_footprint} false, a point robot behind an inflation radius of $0.45$~m, at
least the $0.22$~m radius of the circular simulated robot; a robot with a non-circular
footprint needs \texttt{consider\_footprint} true and its own validation.

\textbf{Success criterion.} A trial \emph{reached} the goal if the Gazebo ground-truth
position of the robot is within $0.5$~m of the goal at the end of the drive and the chassis
never touched a wall (distance at least $0.01$~m in every sample). Nav2's own
\texttt{SUCCEEDED} is recorded and not used.

\textbf{Analysis and decision rule.} Counts per cell with Wilson 95\% intervals;
two-sided Fisher exact tests \cite{fisher_exact} for C4 against C3 (s1, s3, s4 separately), C2
against C1 (s1, s3), and C4 on s3 and on s4 each against C4 on s1 (transfer): seven contrasts at
a level of $0.05$, no correction, no pooling over scenarios. Only two of them decide the
branch, each once. The hypothesis on a pocket is \emph{yes} if and only if the Fisher $p$ of C4 against
C3 on it is below $0.05$ and C4 reached the goal more often. The branch \emph{demonstrated} requires
yes on s1 and yes on s4; s3 does not change the branch; no on s1 is \emph{not demonstrated}
whatever s4 shows. Requiring both s1 and s4 to be \emph{yes} is an intersection-union test, so
the probability of the branch \emph{demonstrated} is at most $0.05$ without a correction whenever
at least one of the two pockets has no effect. Because s1 is a development scenario, its
\emph{yes} is not confirmatory, and the evidence of transfer is the s4 contrast alone
($p=0.0152$). With six trials a cell, C4 at 5 of 6 against C3 at 0 of 6 gives $p=0.0152$ and
4 of 6 gives $p=0.0606$. The comparison of C2 with C1, the control, the transfer contrasts and all
time comparisons are descriptive. The wording for each branch was copied before the trials from the
design advice into the pre-registration.

\textbf{Amendments and deviations.} Every amendment is dated in the pre-registration. We list them
all, because one was written after counted trials had run.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Amendment 1 (2026-10-18, before any trial).} The author accepted additions (a) to (d)
  as the shipped algorithm and added \texttt{s4\_ltrap}, with C3 and C4 on it, because \texttt{s3\_ctrap}
  has a twin in the development family; the plan grew from 76 to 88 trials and the decision rule moved
  the claim of transfer to s4.
\item \emph{Driver import failure (2026-10-22, before any trial).} The first start of Phase A ended at once
  with an \texttt{AttributeError}: the driver imported the analysis script of campaign 1 instead of
  its own. No simulator or trial ran. \emph{Amendment 2} fixed the import by file path and added a
  regression test; the analysis rules, plan, worlds, controller and thresholds did not change.
\item \emph{Provenance refusal (2026-10-22, after the first counted trial).} After the first trial
  (C4 on s1, repetition 0) the provenance check refused the next one, because Python had written a
  bytecode cache into the installed, frozen launch directory. The author deleted the cache and made the
  directory read-only. No frozen file, code, parameter or environment variable changed, and the trial
  stayed counted, because it ran on a verified install.
\item \emph{Amendment 3 (2026-10-22), post hoc.} The stopping rule for a controller crash fired after the
  counted trial \texttt{s4\_ltrap} C3 repetition 0 (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2results}). The amendment
  was written after the rule had fired and changes how the rule reads the evidence: a launched process
  that exits with a non-teardown code is a \emph{teardown crash}, reported and not stopping the
  campaign, only if the process itself logged the termination signal inside the teardown window and
  before the launch logged its death; every other death stays a controller crash. Without the
  amendment the registered rule would have stopped the campaign at that point, and the remaining trials
  would not have run under it. It also departs from the rule ``no source change after trial~1'':
  the analyzer, the driver's two teardown time stamps, the provenance module and the manifest module
  changed; the controller, plan, worlds and parameters did not. The tag moved with it, so trials
  recorded before it carry the earlier freeze identity, which the analysis accepts for exactly that
  earlier set. It was written after 16 counted trials, all at the tag commit
  \texttt{012f4f1}. At that point the author knew these outcomes: on s1, C4 complete at 5 of 6,
  C3 failed in repetitions 0 to 2, C1 0 of 1 and C2 0 of 1; on s3, C1 0 of 1, C2 1 of 1, C3 0 of
  1 and C4 1 of 1; on s4, C3 0 of 1 (the crash trial). No C4 trial on s4 had run. All six C4
  trials on s4 and C3 repetitions 1 to 5 on s4 ran after the amendment, so the s4 contrast
  exists only under the amended rule. The registered rule required stopping the campaign or
  restarting it as attempt 2; we did neither. From \texttt{012f4f1} to \texttt{6a50723}, the diff of
  \texttt{analyze.py} touches the classification of crashes, the identity check that accepts
  the earlier trials, one recorded start time and the report sections; it does not touch the
  outcome logic (reached, contact, cells, contrasts, decision).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Reading, and what it does not show.} The two contrasts that decide the branch have $p=0.0152$ each,
with five of six against zero of six. A single failure more in the SE cell would have given $p=0.0606$ and no
claim, so the result is as narrow as six repetitions allow, and the Wilson interval of 5 of 6 is
$[0.44,0.97]$. Three results are not significant and are not claims of effect: C4 against C3 on s3
(4 of 6 against 0 of 6, $p=0.0606$; the pre-registered rule marks this ``no'', and the pre-registered sentence
requires that both be reported); C2 against C1 on s1 (3 of 6 against 0 of 6, $p=0.1818$) and on s3 (2 of 6 against
0 of 6, $p=0.4545$). In particular the live data do not show that SE-MPPI helps inside Nav2's default
recovery tree beyond what six trials can resolve: the direction is favourable and the intervals overlap.
\textbf{Forced stops without a moving body.} No pocket held a moving body, yet the brake
fallback of Sec.~\ref{sec:cbf} (linear velocity, forward or reverse, set to zero for one control
cycle of about $0.1$~s, so in a pocket it also stops a reverse out in that cycle) fired in the SE pocket trials of s3 and s4. The QP failure counter is $0$ in all 88
trials, so every forced stop was a brake on barrier slack, not on a solver failure. The counts
come from the last diagnostics message of each trial, which the controller publishes once per
wall-clock second, so stops in the final second are missing and the counts are lower bounds.
The count of forced-stop cycles at the last
diagnostics message of each trial was, per repetition: s3 C2 $5, 9, 9, 7, 8, 16$; s3 C4
$8, 8, 7, 5, 12, 8$; s4 C4 $1, 1, 0, 6, 0, 2$; and zero in every s1 trial and on \texttt{s0\_open}.
The filter returns the nominal command unchanged when the tracker admits no obstacle, so a forced
stop means that the tracker admitted phantom obstacles: costmap clusters that passed the
\texttt{is\_dynamic} veto, the speed threshold and the radius limit of $1.0$~m. Admissions were not
limited to pockets. The diagnostics field \texttt{cbf\_obstacles}, the number of obstacles passed to
the CBF in the cycle of the message (sampled once per wall-clock second, so the counts are lower
bounds), was above zero in at least one sample in 42 of 46 SE-MPPI trials (evidence trials included),
among them 8 of 12 on \texttt{s0\_open}, with at most 2 obstacles in a sample; forced stops occurred
only on s3 and s4 (post hoc, \texttt{experiments/campaign2/}\allowbreak\texttt{CBF\_ADMISSIONS.md},
from the raw archive). An admitted obstacle that needs no slack can still change the QP's
$(v,\omega)$ without a stop; no diagnostic records whether it did, so we do not know how often the
CBF altered commands. This was
not a designed test of the coordination, and we did not test whether the stops or the admissions
changed any outcome. Deployment warning: a robot can be slowed, turned or stopped by obstacles that
do not exist, in open space as well as in a pocket; read both the forced-stop counter and
\texttt{cbf\_obstacles} of the diagnostics on the target map.

\textbf{Teardown crash.} One process died with a non-teardown exit code, in \texttt{s4\_ltrap}
C3 repetition 0, a stock-controller trial that had already ended in \texttt{NOT\_REACHED\_ABORTED}.
The driver's teardown signal reached the processes $3.0$~s after the drive ended and $4.0$~s before

bond-timeout path of the localization lifecycle manager (``SERVER map\_server IS DOWN'',
``Terminating bond timer''); a \texttt{SIGSEGV} message from the image library's signal handler,
which carries no timestamp; and the exit of the composed container
(\texttt{component\_container\_isolated}) with code $-6$. This is the only such death in the
campaign, and the one on which amendment 3 was decided; that amendment classifies it a teardown
crash, and \texttt{RESULTS.md} lists it per trial. We make no claim about its cause. On a robot, a
crash of the composed container stops all navigation nodes that it hosts; use a respawn policy or a
non-composed bringup.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Simulation only, one host, no real robot.} Every live result is a
  simulated robot in Gazebo on one workstation (WSL2, Ryzen 5 5500GT, 12 threads, simulated
  time; real-time factor $0.93$ to $0.97$). We
  ran no real robot and no second host. The claim is limited to the pre-registered
  wording: counts, Fisher $p$ values and Wilson intervals, on one simulated host.
\item \textbf{Six repetitions per cell.} The decisive contrasts are 5 of 6 against 0 of 6
  ($p=0.0152$), the smallest effect that clears the pre-registered level at this
  size; one more failure would have given $p=0.0606$. The Wilson interval of 5 of 6 is
  $[0.44, 0.97]$. The comparison with Nav2's default recovery tree (C2 against C1: 3 of 6
  against 0 of 6 on s1, 2 of 6 against 0 of 6 on s3) is not significant, so the live data do not
  establish an advantage inside the default tree. The seven contrasts use no correction for
  multiple tests; only two decide the branch.
\item \textbf{The additions were found on the development family.} The four additions of
  Sec.~\ref{sec:extras} were worked on until the 56 development geometries passed, and the
  advised design without (a) and (b) passes 14 to 18 of 56 in the three archived confirmation sweeps
  (19 in an earlier, unarchived sweep). The 56 of 56 is a development result. MPPI's
  noise cannot be seeded from the test, so each sweep is one independent process, and a geometry's
  outcome without the additions depended on its position in the sweep.
  The mirror benchmark without the additions gives a lower success on the trap families than with them
  (Table~\ref{tab:bench2d-arms}; on U-trap $58\%$ against $80\%$, descriptive): the advised design
  already lifts U-trap success from $0\%$, and the additions add to it.
\item \textbf{Held-out status.} \texttt{s1\_utrap} is the development scenario, and static gap tests at its
  stall pose helped set the additions. \texttt{s3\_ctrap} has a near mirror twin in the
  development family and is not decision-bearing; its result (4 of 6 against 0 of 6,
  $p=0.0606$) is not significant. The claim of transfer rests on one pocket, \texttt{s4\_ltrap},
  which lies outside the family by depth (by $0.20$~m, 11 percent) and shape but inside its
  span in width and rotation. One pocket shows that the controller escaped a geometry that the
  design did not see; it does not show that it does so for pockets in general.
\item \textbf{Nav2's own report and the localization estimate.} 13 of 88 trials ended in Nav2
  \texttt{SUCCEEDED} with the robot $0.54$ to $1.66$~m from the goal in the ground truth
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2results}). Wheel odometry and the AMCL estimate drift after the turns and
  reversals of an escape, and the goal checker works from that estimate. We count these trials as not reached,
  which makes the live counts conservative, but a deployment that relies on the estimate would
  see the controller report success before the robot is at the goal. We did not isolate the cause.
  Reached trials drifted too: in the 21 SE pocket trials that reached the goal (19 registered
  and 2 evidence trials), the odometry is
  $0.09$ to $0.54$~m and the AMCL estimate $0.06$ to $0.54$~m from the ground truth at the end of
  the drive, so the reached count mixes escape with end-of-drive localization error.
\item \textbf{The no-valid-control update cannot act under Nav2's default failure tolerance.} With
  \texttt{failure\_tolerance} $0$, as in all configurations of campaign 2, the controller server resets every
  controller when the optimizer throws, so the detector update on a throw (defect 2 of
  Sec.~\ref{sec:history}) acts only when the server tolerates the failure (a tolerance of $-1$, or a
  positive tolerance that has not yet run out, after which Jazzy's server aborts with
  \texttt{PatienceExceeded}). Campaign 2 did not exercise it, and
  its pass rule in the headless test requires zero throws.
\item \textbf{Pre-registration is self-hosted.} The pre-registration was not deposited with a
  third party. The freeze dates can be checked only through the local commit history
  (\texttt{ac0a4eb} for the freeze, \texttt{c6a144e} and \texttt{012f4f1} for amendment 2,
  \texttt{6a50723} for amendment 3); the tag moved twice. The reader must trust that history.
\item \textbf{Option B is out of scope.} The controller does not emit its own command when
  every rollout collides, for example when the footprint is already inside the inflated band. Then the
  optimizer throws and the detector runs. Only when the server tolerates the failure (a tolerance of $-1$,
  or a positive tolerance that has not yet run out) is an escape path handed to MPPI, in the next cycle; under the campaign's tolerance of $0$ the server resets the controller in the
  same cycle. In either case MPPI cannot produce a command in the cycle that throws. No headless run and no campaign-2 trial threw, with reversing allowed.
\item \textbf{The post hoc amendment.} Amendment 3 was written after a stopping rule fired once, and the
  crash it concerns happened in the shutdown of a stock-controller trial. It changes how that rule reads the
  evidence and departs from the rule of no source change after trial~1. Its effect is that the remaining trials ran,
  among them every C4 trial on s4, the pocket that carries the claim of transfer.
  The cause of the crash is not known. The crash scan also does not read the launch log of an interrupted
  or incomplete attempt, a gap older than the amendment.
\item \textbf{Released defaults and loosened tolerances were not evaluated live.} The released
  parameter file uses $20$~Hz, $56$ steps of $0.05$~s and batch $1000$; the campaign used $10$~Hz,
  $28$ steps of $0.1$~s and batch $1500$. The stall window is $30$ cycles in both, so it is
  $3$~s in the campaign and $1.5$~s at $20$~Hz; the watchdog and the re-selection period are
  also counted in cycles, so all three stretch when the loop misses its rate. We did not evaluate
  the released defaults live; on this host a $20$~Hz configuration with batch $2000$ ran at about
  $5$~Hz and lost the lifecycle bond (Sec.~\ref{sec:integration}). The
  campaign also loosened \texttt{transform\_tolerance} ($0.5$~s) and \texttt{source\_timeout}
  ($5.0$~s); do not copy them to a robot. The only \texttt{collision\_monitor} polygon was
  disabled, so no \texttt{collision\_monitor} zone was active in any live result.
\item \textbf{Builds.} The live results come from the correct RoboStack build of the MPPI library
  (Table~\ref{tab:builds}). Some distributed binaries roll trajectories out in the wrong direction; the
  July 2026 runs and 4 trials of campaign 1 used one, and we do not use them. The check the plugin carries covers the batch
  rollout integration, the known defect, and not every function of the library, and it guards only \texttt{SafeEscapeController}. Run
  \texttt{check\_mppi\_rollout} on every target install (apt binaries are untested), and never
  set \texttt{se\_enforce\_rollout\_check} to false on a moving robot. Humble's
  distribution was not evaluated: every reported result comes from the Jazzy stack or the 2D mirror.
  The repository README records that the plugin builds on Humble behind a compile-time switch, that
  Humble's controller server never calls \texttt{reset()}, so a goal end does not clear the escape
  memory, and that the RoboStack Humble MPPI builds it checked roll out in the wrong direction.
\item \textbf{Mirror versus controller.} The mirror transcribes the shipped escape, but it differs
  from the C++ controller in its progress signal with simulated replans, its analytic circular
  geometry, its cost, and in giving the CBF and the TTC the true obstacle states, so the costmap tracker is
  not exercised (Appendix). The $1{,}200$ trials are $200$ seeded worlds under six configs, not independent
  scenarios. Success is tested with paired McNemar tests, but the continuous metrics use unpaired
  Mann--Whitney tests conditioned on success, so they are descriptive.
\item \textbf{Coordination benefit is unmeasured, not established.} The escape--safety
  coordination (C3) has the conditional invariance property of Sec.~\ref{sec:coordination} but
  produced no outcome difference over an independent escape+CBF stack in the mirror
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:evsf}), and campaign 2 does not test it: its pockets hold no moving body. The tracker
  nevertheless admitted phantom obstacles in 42 of 46 SE-MPPI trials, including 8 of 12 on the
  trap-free \texttt{s0\_open}, and the brake fallback forced stops on s3 and s4 only (up to $16$
  cycles in one trial; Sec.~\ref{sec:c2results}). Whether admissions without a stop altered the
  commands was not logged. That was not a designed test of the coordination. Deployment warning: a
  robot can be stopped or deflected by obstacles that do not exist, in open space as well as in a
  pocket. The idea of adapting the CBF gain online is not new
  \cite{kim_adaptive_cbf,br_mppi,rt_cbf}. Demonstrating an outcome-level benefit requires a
  regime where the $\alpha$ choice decides which escapes are admitted.
\item \textbf{Braking is not a safe fallback when cornered.} In the cramped narrow-dynamic pocket the
  infeasible-QP fallback ($v=0$) leaves the robot in the path of a sweeping mover, and the CBF configs
  measured a \emph{higher} collision rate than their no-CBF counterparts ($18\%\!\to\!32\%$;
  Sec.~\ref{sec:bench2d}). A cornered robot needs an evasive fallback, not a stop.
\item \textbf{Look-ahead-point vs.\ full-body safety.} The Proposition of
  Sec.~\ref{sec:coordination} concerns the look-ahead point $P$, not the full robot body. Body safety is
  therefore \textbf{approximate}: the offset $L\approx r$ places $P$ at the robot's leading edge, and the
  costmap obstacle critic covers the residual; in the live campaign no \texttt{collision\_monitor}
  zone was active, so it covered nothing. A full-body guarantee
  would require inflating the margin by $L$, which we avoid because it over-constrains sub-meter gaps and
  would degrade the success rate. On the \texttt{dynamic} family the look-ahead filter left body-grazing
  collisions essentially unchanged ($20\%\!\to\!16\%$, descriptive).
\item \textbf{Sampled-data and slack caveats.} Invariance between samples is assumed in the Proposition,
  not proven, and the shared slack couples the rows of one solve; we report slack usage instead of
  claiming an unconditional guarantee. Static structure is outside the barrier, and in cycles with
  $\delta>\epsilon$ the braking fallback replaces the QP command.
\item \textbf{Constant-velocity tracker.} The dynamic-obstacle model evaluated in this paper is a
  replaceable constant-velocity baseline; rotating or accelerating agents are mispredicted and prediction
  uncertainty is unquantified, and an obstacle that leaves the tracked, unpruned set $\mathcal{O}$ also
  leaves the barrier (Sec.~\ref{sec:coordination}). The controller that ran live carries an occupancy-based
  static/dynamic classification, persistent tracks with horizon prediction, and an online conformal bound
  that inflates the CBF effective radius. The campaign parameter file does not set
  \texttt{se\_classify\_static}, \texttt{se\_predict\_horizon} or \texttt{se\_conformal}; their code
  defaults are true, so all three were on in every live SE trial. They are the subject of follow-up work
  (SE-Predict) and none is present in the mirror behind the quantitative results reported here. C4 and the
  tracker description of this paper hold for this configuration only.
\item \textbf{Baselines and scale.} We ran no full-scale 3D physics benchmark (BARN, DynaBARN, HuNavSim \cite{barn,dynabarn,hunavsim})
  and no run of DRPA-MPPI, GP-MPPI, a gap-based planner or an adaptive-gain CBF
  \cite{drpa_mppi,gp_mppi,safer_gap,kim_adaptive_cbf}; we make no comparison to them. The only
  stack-level baseline is Nav2's default recovery tree around stock and SE-MPPI in campaign 2.
\item \textbf{TTC, real time, localization.} TTC is a one-dimensional approximation; per-call QP cost grows
  with the obstacle budget (capped by clearance pruning); the tracker stamps a costmap frame in the
  control cycle that first sees it, so its velocity estimates carry the jitter of that rounding and, on a
  robot, the costmap latency; localization in symmetric or narrow maps can jump
  and perturb progress estimation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

SE-MPPI is a system that packages local-minima escape and control-barrier-function safety, two
problems that several prior works also address together outside Nav2, in a single Nav2 controller
plugin: a shared entrapment signal drives an escape that hands MPPI a path to an opening and an
output-time CBF filter, and a coordinated gain raises the barrier's class-$\mathcal{K}$ gain during
escape, which, by a standard argument for time-varying gains, keeps the look-ahead point's safe set with
respect to the tracked dynamic obstacles forward invariant, assuming the barrier inequality holds between
control updates and the QP uses no slack.

The first version did not reach the goal live: 0 of 44 valid trials on a correct build. Four defects
explain it (one is inferred, not logged), and the repaired controller carries four additions to the advised escape, two needed together for the
development sweeps to pass (ablated jointly, confounded with position in the sweep) and two safeguards for other configurations; all four are
disclosed, with a joint ablation of the first two. In the pre-registered campaign on one simulated
host (one post hoc amendment to its crash-stop rule, after which all s4 SE-MPPI trials ran), SE-MPPI without recoveries reached the goal in 5 of 6 on s1 (Wilson 95\% interval $[0.44, 0.97]$)
and 5 of 6 on the held-out s4; stock without recoveries in 0 of 6 on each; with Nav2's default recovery
tree, on s1, stock 0 of 6 and SE-MPPI 3 of 6.
The Fisher exact $p$ of the two decisive contrasts is $0.0152$. s1 is the development scenario;
s4 carries the claim. The third pocket, s3, gave 4 of 6 against 0 of 6 ($p=0.0606$), which is not
significant. The result does not rest on a real robot, a second host or more than six repetitions per cell.

A randomized, paired $1{,}200$-trial benchmark on a Python 2D mirror of the shipped algorithm shows the
escape raising success on the U-trap family from $0\%$ to $80\%$, and to $58\%$ without the four
additions, while the escape--safety coordination is indistinguishable from an independent escape+CBF
stack there, which we report as a null. We addressed three integration faults that surfaced in the
live stack -- static-wall CBF infeasibility (the wall freeze stopped; phantom admissions remained),
host-matched compute budgeting, and an xtensor ABI mismatch -- and we guard against a defective
distributed MPPI binary. The remaining steps are a real
robot, more repetitions and pockets, a test of the coordination in a regime where it can matter, and a full-scale
physics benchmark against the baselines of Sec.~\ref{sec:related}. The codebase also contains a
prediction stack and a multi-robot layer that are the subject of separate work and are not evaluated
here; the prediction features ran at their code defaults in campaign~2 (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\textbf{2D mirror versus the Nav2 controller.} The 2D mirror
(\texttt{experiments/prototype/se\_mppi\_v2.py} for the shipped escape, driven by
\texttt{experiments/benchmark2d/rollout.py}) follows the controller's formulas but differs in the following
documented ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Progress signal.} Progress is the arc length along an anchor path, with straight replans
  simulated once a second, as in the controller; the design-stage mirror of Appendix~\ref{app:v1} used
  the best distance to the goal instead.
\item \emph{Geometry.} Obstacles, including walls built from chains of discs, are circles; repulsion
  distances and ray lengths are computed analytically against them rather than on a costmap distance field.
  The repulsion has the same Khatib form but other parameters and distances: $d_0=0.8$~m and
  $\eta=0.3$, the distance to the surface of each disc (clamped at $0.02$~m), and the sum over
  discs of the per-disc horizon mean; the controller uses $d_0=0.6$~m, $\eta=1$ and the
  distance to the nearest inscribed-band cell, without a sum.
\item \emph{Obstacle knowledge.} The CBF and TTC receive the true positions and velocities of the moving
  obstacles, so the tracker of Sec.~\ref{sec:tracking} is not exercised.
\item \emph{Costs.} The MPPI cost is a goal-distance, path-progress, and obstacle-proximity cost rather than
  Nav2's critic stack, and the parameters differ from the released controller's.
\item \emph{Stack.} Nav2's progress checker and behavior-tree recoveries are not modeled.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Data and parameters.} The full parameter set of the live campaign is
\texttt{experiments/sim/nav2\_se\_campaign2.yaml}; the released defaults are in
\texttt{src/nav2\_se\_controller/config/}\allowbreak\texttt{nav2\_se\_controller\_params.yaml}.

\subsection{Mechanism validation (standalone 2D)}
\label{sec:mechanism}

math (repulsion, gap ray cast, look-ahead CBF-QP via OSQP \cite{osqp}, $\alpha$ modulation + TTC override,
monotone-progress entrapment) as a 2D unicycle and ran three scenarios. This code is the design-stage mirror; in
these three scenes its CBF sees all obstacles, including the static wall discs.
